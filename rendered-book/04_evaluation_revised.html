7  Comparative Evaluation

This chapter brings together the results from all Stage 2 models - classical embeddings (Word2Vec, FastText), fine-tuned compact LLMs for sentiment classification (TinyLlama 1.1B, Gemma 2 2B), a fine-tuned LLM for topic classification (Qwen2.5-1.5B), and a domain-adapted transformer embedding model (Gemma Embedding 300M) - and evaluates them on a common basis across three dimensions: classification performance, computational cost, and interpretability.

7.1 A Note on Label Quality

All sentiment models are evaluated against labels derived from cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest, a RoBERTa model trained on Twitter data, while our corpus consists of long-form AI media articles. The F1 scores therefore reflect agreement with the labelling model rather than true sentiment accuracy, which places a practical ceiling on all results and their interpretation.

Topic classification models are evaluated against labels produced by an upstream BERT-based topic modelling pipeline. These labels are algorithmically derived, not manually annotated. The reported metrics therefore measure agreement with the upstream topic model rather than validated ground truth, which is an important caveat when interpreting absolute scores.

7.3 Key Findings — Sentiment Classification

Classical embeddings are surprisingly competitive. Word2Vec achieves 0.752 Macro-F1 - only 0.009 below the best model (Gemma 2 2B, 0.761) - while training in 2 minutes on a laptop CPU with a CO2 footprint 625 times smaller than Gemma 2. For this specific task and dataset, classical methods deliver near-equivalent performance at a fraction of the cost.

Model size yields diminishing returns. Doubling the parameter count from TinyLlama (1.1B) to Gemma 2 (2B) improved Macro-F1 by only 0.004. The marginal gain does not justify the 2.4x increase in training time and energy consumption.

All models struggle equally with minority classes. Negative and positive sentiment both score around F1 = 0.72 across all approaches, while neutral consistently scores ~0.83. This pattern reflects the class imbalance in the dataset (neutral = 61%, positive = 24%, negative = 15%) rather than model-specific weaknesses.

The label quality ceiling matters. Since sentiment labels were generated by a Twitter-trained RoBERTa model applied to long-form journalism, the practical upper bound for any model on this task is well below 1.0. The narrow spread of results (0.745–0.761) across very different architectures suggests all models are approaching this ceiling.

7.4 Topic Classification — Qwen2.5-1.5B

Qwen2.5-1.5B was fine-tuned for topic classification using LoRA adapters (4-bit quantisation, r=16, 2 epochs on an A100 GPU). Because the task and labels differ from the sentiment experiments, it is more appropriate to compare Qwen against the classical topic classification baselines than against TinyLlama or Gemma 2.

7.4.1 Baseline vs. Fine-Tuned Performance

Fine-tuning produced a very large absolute gain. Before task-specific training, the pretrained Qwen2.5-1.5B model had near-random performance on topic classification: accuracy of 34.3% and a Macro-F1 of only 0.081. After two epochs of LoRA fine-tuning, accuracy rose to 79.4% and Macro-F1 reached 0.720. This delta illustrates how much a domain-general language model must adapt before it can match even simple classical baselines on a structured classification task.

A likely explanation for Qwen’s underperformance relative to classical models is the severe input truncation imposed during training. The median article in the dataset exceeds 1,400 tokens, yet MAX_LENGTH was capped at 512 tokens - meaning fewer than 1% of texts fit within the model’s context window without truncation. Classical embedding models, by contrast, use mean-pooled representations computed over the full document, losing no content. This structural advantage may explain why Word2Vec and FastText - which learned compact, domain-specific co-occurrence patterns - outperformed a much larger fine-tuned LLM on this particular task.

7.4.3 Training Dynamics

The Qwen loss curves showed clean, stable convergence: training loss declined sharply from ~5.91 in the first hundred steps to ~0.39 by the end of epoch 2. Validation Macro-F1 rose from 0.877 at the end of epoch 1 to 0.906 at the end of epoch 2, with validation loss still decreasing (0.274 → 0.247). This indicates the model had not yet plateaued after two epochs and would likely benefit from a third. Unlike the sentiment models (trained for 3 epochs), Qwen was stopped early due to compute constraints.

7.4.4 Label Quality Caveat for Topic

Topic labels were produced by an upstream BERT-based topic modelling pipeline, not by human annotators. The reported metrics therefore measure agreement with that upstream model rather than true topic accuracy. This is particularly relevant when comparing Qwen against the classical baselines: both are learning to predict the same algorithmically derived labels, but the classical models may exploit co-occurrence shortcuts that the topic model itself relies on, giving them a structural edge that would not necessarily generalise to manually validated topics.

7.5 Embedding Model Evaluation — Gemma Embedding 300M

The experiments above concern supervised classification. A separate line of work explored whether domain-adapted transformer embeddings could improve downstream retrieval quality for a RAG pipeline. This section documents that experiment and its findings.

7.5.1 Pretrained Baseline: Strong Out-of-the-Box Performance

Before any fine-tuning, the pretrained gemma-embedding-300m was evaluated by training a logistic regression probe on its frozen embeddings. The results are striking:

This demonstrates that a well-pretrained transformer embedding model, used purely as a frozen feature extractor, is a competitive alternative to task-specific fine-tuning.

7.5.2 Fine-Tuning Outcome: Representation Regression

The SimCSE fine-tuning degraded performance on both tasks. The topic classification probe dropped by 0.319 Macro-F1 points. Intrinsic evaluation (cosine similarity gap between synthetic positive and negative article pairs) confirmed the same direction: the positive- negative gap decreased after fine-tuning, meaning the embedding space became less well-ordered rather than more so.

Importantly, intrinsic and extrinsic evaluations moved in the same direction, validating the use of the cosine similarity gap as a cheap proxy for downstream embedding quality.

The three most likely causes of the regression are:

  1. Learning rate too aggressive. 1e-5 over 436,000 samples represents a very large cumulative parameter displacement for a model that is already strongly pretrained contrastively. A rate of 1e-6 or lower would have been safer.
  2. Too many gradient steps. One full epoch over 436k chunks yields ~6,800 steps. Domain adaptation should be a gentle nudge — limiting to ~10-15% of the corpus with a hard max_steps=2000 cap would have reduced the risk of overwriting general-purpose representations.
  3. Homogeneous corpus. AI/tech journalism shares a consistent register and vocabulary, making in-batch SimCSE negatives trivially easy. This produces shallow gradients rather than genuine structural learning.

7.5.3 Corrected Recipe for Future Runs

Based on the lessons from this experiment, the recommended configuration for a second attempt is:

from sentence_transformers.losses import CachedMultipleNegativesRankingLoss

args = SentenceTransformerTrainingArguments(
    ...
    learning_rate=5e-7,           # 20× lower than original
    max_steps=2_000,              # hard cap — ~5% of original step count
    per_device_train_batch_size=128,
    batch_sampler=BatchSamplers.NO_DUPLICATES,
    ...
)
loss = CachedMultipleNegativesRankingLoss(model, mini_batch_size=8)

# Also: freeze the bottom half of transformer layers to prevent
# catastrophic forgetting of general-purpose representations.

This two-phase strategy — domain familiarisation (Phase 1, SimCSE) followed by supervised retrieval training on query/passage pairs (Phase 2) — remains sound in principle. The failure was in the implementation of Phase 1, not the overall design.

7.6 Conclusion

For AI trend detection in media texts, classical Word2Vec embeddings combined with logistic regression offer the best cost-performance ratio for both sentiment and topic classification. Fine-tuned LLMs provide marginal improvements on sentiment at substantially higher computational cost and carbon footprint, and did not outperform classical models on topic classification under the conditions tested.

The most significant finding from the broader set of experiments is the performance of pretrained gemma-embedding-300m as a frozen feature extractor: it outperformed every other model on both tasks without any task-specific training. This suggests that investing in a high-quality general-purpose embedding model may be more productive than fine-tuning smaller models for specific tasks, at least when labelled data is limited or labels are algorithmically derived.

The choice between approaches should be guided by the deployment context: if inference speed, energy efficiency, and interpretability matter, classical models are the pragmatic choice. If a unified embedding for both retrieval and classification is needed, a strong pretrained encoder like gemma-embedding-300m is a compelling option. Task-specific fine-tuning of compact LLMs is a viable path only when the accuracy gains justify the infrastructure cost and when input truncation does not structurally disadvantage the model relative to baselines.

7.7 Insight Extraction: What Do the Embeddings Reveal About AI Trends?

Beyond classification performance, we use the best-performing classical model (Word2Vec) to examine the thematic structure of AI discourse. By projecting document embeddings into 2D with UMAP and colouring by the topic labels derived in Stage 1, we can directly inspect how topics cluster in the learned embedding space - and what this reveals about the organisation of AI media coverage.

Show/Hide code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import umap
from pathlib import Path
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

_cwd = Path.cwd()
REPO_ROOT  = _cwd if (_cwd / "data").exists() else _cwd.parents[1]
STAGE2_DIR = REPO_ROOT / "data/processed/stage2"

# Load data and model
train = pd.read_parquet(STAGE2_DIR / "train.parquet")
test  = pd.read_parquet(STAGE2_DIR / "test.parquet")
train["tokens"] = train["tokens"].apply(eval)
test["tokens"]  = test["tokens"].apply(eval)

w2v_model = Word2Vec.load(str(STAGE2_DIR / "word2vec.model"))

def mean_pooling(tokens, model):
    vectors = [model.wv[t] for t in tokens if t in model.wv]
    return np.mean(vectors, axis=0) if vectors else np.zeros(model.vector_size)

# Combine train and test for a fuller picture
all_df = pd.concat([train, test], ignore_index=True)
all_df = all_df.dropna(subset=["topic_label"]).reset_index(drop=True)
X_all  = np.array([mean_pooling(t, w2v_model) for t in all_df["tokens"]])

# UMAP projection
reducer = umap.UMAP(n_components=2, random_state=42, n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1)
proj    = reducer.fit_transform(X_all)

# Plot coloured by topic
topics  = all_df["topic_label"].tolist()
unique_topics = sorted(set(topics))
palette = plt.cm.get_cmap("tab10", len(unique_topics))
color_map = {t: palette(i) for i, t in enumerate(unique_topics)}
colors  = [color_map[t] for t in topics]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(14, 9))
for topic in unique_topics:
    mask = [t == topic for t in topics]
    ax.scatter(proj[mask, 0], proj[mask, 1],
               c=[color_map[topic]], s=3, alpha=0.4, label=topic)

ax.legend(markerscale=4, fontsize=8, loc="upper right",
          framealpha=0.8, title="Topic")
ax.set_title("Word2Vec Document Embeddings - UMAP projection coloured by Topic (Stage 1 labels)")
ax.set_xticks([])
ax.set_yticks([])
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The projection reveals a clear thematic organisation of AI media discourse that goes beyond what classification metrics can capture. Search, SEO & Marketing (grey) forms two distinct isolated clusters outside the main body, reflecting its highly domain-specific vocabulary around search engines and digital marketing. Stocks & Market Ratings (yellow-green) separates cleanly to the right, and Quantum Computing (pink) clusters at the bottom right of the main body - both topics carry specialised terminology with little overlap with general AI coverage. Consumer Tech (red) appears as a compact cluster above the main body, while Energy, Power & Robotics (purple) sits at the upper left periphery. At the core, AI News & Tech (General) dominates the dense centre, with ML Models & Training Data heavily overlapping - consistent with the difficulty all models had separating these two classes in classification.

Crucially, this structure was learned entirely from word co-occurrence statistics, without any topic supervision. The fact that Word2Vec recovers the broad organisation of AI discourse unsupervised demonstrates that classical embeddings trained on domain-specific text capture genuine thematic structure - making them a practical tool for tracking how AI topics relate and evolve over time.
